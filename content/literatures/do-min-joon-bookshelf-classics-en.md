# Books a 400-Year-Old Alien Reads — Four Korean Classics from Do Min-joon's Bookshelf

![Open book and tea cup on a windowsill bathed in spring natural light](/images/hexagons/my-love-from-the-star/k-literature/hero-book-tea-windowsill.jpg)
*Photo by Arsonela K on Pexels*

## Why Do Min-joon's Bookshelf Matters

In [My Love from the Star](/content/dramas/my-love-from-the-star-en.html), Do Min-joon (Kim Soo-hyun) is an alien who arrived in Joseon-era Korea in 1609. Over more than four centuries on the Korean peninsula, the most visible evidence of his accumulated life is a massive bookshelf. Throughout the drama, his study is shown on screen, and on those shelves sit *Guunmong* (구운몽), *Cheongnogjip* (청록집), and Korean translations of foreign novels.

These are not random props. When you consider why a being with 400 years of memory would keep *these particular* books, their themes align precisely with Do Min-joon's arc. This article selects four titles from that bookshelf as an entry point for global readers discovering Korean classical literature.

## 1. Guunmong (The Cloud Dream of the Nine) — Kim Man-jung (1687)

In brief: A Buddhist monk named Seongjin experiences an entire worldly life — love, ambition, glory — as a dream, then wakes to question the meaning of enlightenment.

The Do Min-joon connection: For Do Min-joon, 400 years on Earth mirrors Seongjin's dream-life in *Guunmong*. Every relationship he has formed has eventually dissolved. Knowing that all bonds are impermanent yet choosing to open his heart to Chun Song-yi — this may be why a 17th-century novel still sits on his shelf four centuries later.

For the global reader: *Guunmong* was one of the earliest Korean classical novels translated into English. James Scarth Gale published it as *The Cloud Dream of the Nine* in 1922. Its dual-reality narrative structure — a dream world nested inside the waking world — invites comparison with Borges or Calderón's *Life Is a Dream*, making it accessible to readers already familiar with Western literary traditions.

- Author: Kim Man-jung (1637–1692), pen name Seopo
- First edition: c. 1687 (manuscript circulation; woodblock editions followed)
- English translation: James Scarth Gale, *The Cloud Dream of the Nine* (1922)

## 2. Cheongnogjip (The Green Deer Anthology) — Park Mok-wol, Jo Ji-hun, Park Du-jin (1946)

In brief: A joint poetry collection by three poets published just after Korea's liberation from Japanese colonial rule — a landmark of modern Korean nature poetry.

The Do Min-joon connection: The mountains and seasons these three poets celebrated are the same landscapes Do Min-joon watched over hundreds of years. Humans experience seasons within a single lifetime; Do Min-joon saw the same mountains pass through hundreds of springs and winters. For him, the nature lyricism of *Cheongnogjip* was not nostalgia but a record of time itself.

For the global reader: *Cheongnogjip* is the first poetry collection mentioned in any discussion of modern Korean verse. The three poets are known as the "Cheongnog-pa" (Green Deer School), occupying a unique position between colonial-era resistance poetry and post-liberation ideological literature with their commitment to pure nature poetry. Representative works include Park Mok-wol's "The Wanderer," Jo Ji-hun's "The Monk's Dance," and Park Du-jin's "The Sun."

- Authors: Park Mok-wol (1915–1978), Jo Ji-hun (1920–1968), Park Du-jin (1916–1998)
- Publisher: Eulyoo Publishing (을유문화사, 1946)
- English translations: Individual poems appear in multiple Korean literature anthologies in English (including translations by Brother Anthony of Taizé)

## 3. The Story of Hong Gildong — Heo Gyun (early 17th century)

In brief: Hong Gildong, the illegitimate son of a nobleman, rebels against the rigid class discrimination of Joseon society, becomes a righteous outlaw, and ultimately founds his own ideal state.

The Do Min-joon connection: Do Min-joon arrived in Joseon in 1609; Heo Gyun died in 1618. They were contemporaries. If Do Min-joon owns *The Story of Hong Gildong*, it is not a literary preference — it is a record of his own era, a text capturing the injustice he personally witnessed.

For the global reader: *The Story of Hong Gildong* is recognized as the first novel written in the Korean alphabet (hangul). While its adventure structure invites comparison with Robin Hood narratives, its core is a critique of Joseon's discriminatory system between legitimate and illegitimate sons. Hong Gildong remains an icon of righteous rebellion in modern Korean popular culture.

- Author: Heo Gyun (1569–1618)
- Date of composition: Early 17th century (exact year unknown)
- English translation: Minsoo Kang, *The Story of Hong Gildong* (Penguin Classics, 2016)

## 4. The Miraculous Journey of Edward Tulane — Kate DiCamillo (Korean translation)

In brief: A porcelain rabbit named Edward, separated from his owner, passes through many hands and gradually learns what it means to love.

The Do Min-joon connection: This is not a Korean classic but the Korean translation of a 2006 American children's novel. Yet its presence on Do Min-joon's shelf is significant. Edward Tulane transforms from a being incapable of emotion into one who understands love — the exact trajectory of an alien who spent 400 years avoiding human attachment. Korean viewers read this book as Do Min-joon's quiet self-confession.

For the global reader: The Korean edition of Kate DiCamillo's *The Miraculous Journey of Edward Tulane* (2006) was published by Biryongso (비룡소), translated by Kim Gyeong-mi. Following the broadcast of *My Love from the Star*, the book reportedly saw a sharp spike in sales at Korean bookstores.

- Original author: Kate DiCamillo (2006)
- Korean publisher: Biryongso (비룡소)
- Korean translator: Kim Gyeong-mi (김경미)

## What Do Min-joon's Bookshelf Tells Us

The four books converge on a single theme: the moment when a being who knows all relationships are finite decides to love anyway. Seongjin in *Guunmong* wakes from his dream knowing every bond was an illusion, yet does not deny the meaning of the experience. Hong Gildong dreams of an impossible world. Edward Tulane learns to love only after repeated loss.

Before Do Min-joon met Chun Song-yi, he may have already been rehearsing his choice through these books.
